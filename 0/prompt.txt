1. Stage 1 — the scan becomes a shape. A CT scan is a stack of grayscale slices. Bone is much brighter than everything around it, so software (TotalSegmentator) picks out the bone pixels and turns them into a 3D surface. You then throw most of it away: you only keep the section of the tibia where the plate will sit. Smaller mesh, faster everything downstream. Stage 2 — Devin writes a program that draws the plate. This is the key move. Devin doesn't sculpt anything by hand. It writes Python that, when run, produces a plate: "follow this curve along the bone, sweep this cross-section along it, put six screw holes at these positions, taper the thickness from 3.5 mm to 2 mm." The plate is the output of the code. Change a line of code, get a different plate. Stage 3 — the verifier grades it. This is where the loop gets its teeth. The verifier takes the plate, chops it into a few hundred thousand tiny tetrahedra, applies the loads a real tibia sees when someone walks, and computes the stress in every one of them. It then checks a list: is the peak stress safely below the yield limit? Is the plate too stiff? Too heavy? Does it collide with the bone? Do the screws exit where they shouldn't? Can a printer actually make it? It returns numbers, not opinions. Stage 4 — the verdict comes back as instructions, not just a score. "Failed: peak stress 940 MPa at the third screw hole, safety factor 1.4, need 2.5." Devin now edits the code and runs again. Nobody is watching. This repeats until everything passes. Stage 5 — out come real files. The 3D model, the printer instructions, and a report showing the simulated standard test results.

3. Why the split matters The verifier lives outside. If the physics setup sat in the same repo Devin edits, Devin would eventually "fix" a failing check by softening it — lower the load, relax the threshold, change the material. That's not malice, it's just what agents do when a test blocks them. So loads, boundary conditions, material, and pass thresholds are frozen behind an API. Devin can ask for a score. It cannot change how the score is computed. Devin's machine is a workshop, not a courtroom. On its own box it can experiment freely — render pictures, run quick approximate checks, try things. None of that counts. Only what comes back from the outside service goes in the record. Two different kinds of thinking, split between two tools. Devin decides shape questions: should there be a rib here, should the head split into two columns, should the plate be routed further around the bone. A small numeric optimizer decides number questions: exactly how thick, exactly what fillet radius. The optimizer can try 200 variations for the cost of one Devin turn. That's what keeps the whole thing affordable — five Devin decisions instead of forty. Pictures fix the hardest problem. A stress result normally arrives as "node 18437, coordinates (14.2, 3.8, 91.5)." Useless. Devin renders the plate with the stress colored on it, looks at the image, and can say "the hotspot is on the inner edge of the third hole where the plate narrows." That's something you can act on. You also tag each piece of geometry with the code that made it, so the hotspot maps back to an exact variable — the picture explains why, the tag says where.
