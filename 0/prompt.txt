Stage 1 — the scan becomes a shape. A CT scan is a stack of grayscale slices. Bone is much brighter than everything around it, so software (TotalSegmentator) picks out the bone pixels and turns them into a 3D surface. You then throw most of it away: you only keep the section of the tibia where the plate will sit. Smaller mesh, faster everything downstream.

Stage 2 — Devin writes a program that draws the plate. This is the key move. Devin doesn't sculpt anything by hand. It writes Python that, when run, produces a plate: "follow this curve along the bone, sweep this cross-section along it, put six screw holes at these positions, taper the thickness from 3.5 mm to 2 mm." The plate is the output of the code. Change a line of code, get a different plate.

Stage 3 — the verifier grades it. This is where the loop gets its teeth. The verifier takes the plate, chops it into a few hundred thousand tiny tetrahedra, applies the loads a real tibia sees when someone walks, and computes the stress in every one of them. It then checks a list: is the peak stress safely below the yield limit? Is the plate too stiff? Too heavy? Does it collide with the bone? Do the screws exit where they shouldn't? Can a printer actually make it? It returns numbers, not opinions.

Stage 4 — the verdict comes back as instructions, not just a score. "Failed: peak stress 940 MPa at the third screw hole, safety factor 1.4, need 2.5." Devin now edits the code and runs again. Nobody is watching. This repeats until everything passes.

Stage 5 — out come real files. The 3D model, the printer instructions, and a report showing the simulated standard test results.
